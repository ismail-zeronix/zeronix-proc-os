## What engineering software asks of a laptop

Engineering applications push the machine in different ways, and the right laptop depends on which one your team lives in.

- **2D drafting (AutoCAD, DWG files):** needs a fast processor core and enough memory. Most business laptops can do this, but large drawings with many references benefit from 32 GB.
- **3D CAD and BIM (Revit, Civil 3D, SolidWorks):** needs a professional graphics card, 32 GB of memory or more, and a fast SSD. This is where a workstation earns its price.
- **Rendering and visualisation (Enscape, Lumion, V-Ray):** is graphics-heavy. A stronger GPU matters more than the processor.
- **Simulation and analysis (structural, MEP, CFD):** needs many processor cores and a lot of memory.
- **Site work (reviewing drawings, markups, surveys):** needs a light machine, a bright screen and a long battery more than raw power.

Check the software vendor's recommended specification for the programs you use. Many vendors publish a minimum and a recommended spec, and certify certain workstation lines.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Processor | Intel Core Ultra 5 or 7 | Intel Core Ultra 7 or 9, H series | Core Ultra 9 or a desktop workstation chip |
| Memory | 16–32 GB | 32–64 GB | 64 GB or more |
| Storage | 512 GB–1 TB SSD | 1 TB SSD | 1–2 TB SSD plus a drive for projects |
| Graphics | Integrated or entry card | Professional RTX card | Higher-level RTX PRO card |
| Screen | 14–16 inch, 300 nits | 16-inch, colour-accurate | 16-inch, external monitors at the desk |
| Weight | Under 1.5 kg preferred | 1.8–2.2 kg | Tower at the desk |

Two mistakes to avoid: buying **16 GB** for people who run 3D models every day, and buying a laptop with an **integrated graphics chip** for a user who will open BIM models. Both look fine on the day and slow the person down every day after.

## Mobile workstation or business laptop?

A business laptop (ThinkPad T-series, EliteBook, Latitude) is a better fit for engineers who spend the day in documents, email, spreadsheets, PDF markups and 2D drawings. It is lighter, cheaper and has a longer battery.

A mobile workstation adds a professional graphics card, more memory slots, certified drivers for engineering software and a stronger cooling system. It costs more, weighs more and drains the battery faster, but it opens the big model without stuttering.

A simple rule: if the person opens a 3D model every day, give them a workstation. If they only review drawings and write reports, give them a business laptop and save the money.

## Screens, docks and monitors

Engineers draw on large screens. A laptop for the field is also a desk machine, so plan the desk at the same time:

- A **USB-C or Thunderbolt dock** so the laptop connects with one cable to two monitors, a keyboard and a mouse.
- Two **external monitors** of 24 to 27 inches, or one larger monitor, for drawings and models side by side.
- A **full-size keyboard and a proper mouse** for CAD work. A 3D mouse is worth it for SolidWorks and similar software.

## Site conditions: dust, heat and travel

Laptops that go to construction sites face heat, dust and knocks. Look for a laptop tested to a military durability standard such as MIL-STD-810H (the P16s Gen 3 lists this), but remember that a rugged test does not make a laptop waterproof or drop-proof. For harder environments, ask us about rugged models, and use a padded bag.

In the UAE summer, avoid leaving laptops in parked cars. Heat harms batteries and screens faster than any software load.

## Buying for an engineering team: a short checklist

1. List the software each role uses and the largest file they open.
2. Group users: office engineers, design engineers, site engineers and heavy users.
3. Give each group one standard model, with the memory and graphics to match.
4. Decide docks and monitors at the same time as the laptops.
5. Ask for the warranty of the exact part number, and for on-site service on workstations.
6. Ask for a quotation with delivery time, serial numbers on the invoice, and imaging if you need it.
